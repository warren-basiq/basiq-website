# Hero sections

The hero is the only part of a page most visitors read. Nielsen Norman Group's eye-tracking
work puts **57% of viewing time above the fold** and **74% inside the first two screenfuls**,
and their guidance is that a page has roughly **10 seconds** to land its value proposition
before the visitor leaves. Everything below is in service of those ten seconds.

Write the message first and design around it. A hero designed before its message is decided
ends up as a layout with words poured in.

## The five parts, in priority order

1. **Headline.** The single claim. Under 8 words or about 44 characters where the design
   allows; a long headline is a subhead that got promoted.
2. **Subhead.** *One* sentence that adds something the headline could not carry. Never a
   restatement of the headline in longer words.
3. **CTAs.** One primary, at most one secondary. The primary must win on size, contrast and
   position. Two equal buttons is a choice the visitor did not ask to make.
4. **Hero visual.** A real product screen beats an illustration, which beats stock. Show the
   thing working.
5. **Trust signals.** Logos, named customers, a number. Above the fold, not scrolled to.

## Writing the headline

**Name a specific outcome, not a category.** "Close deals 40% faster" outperforms "Sales
automation software." A category name tells the visitor what shelf you sit on, which they
can usually guess, and never tells them why to care.

Three formulas that reliably produce a testable headline:

- **Outcome for audience without pain.** "[Specific outcome] for [audience] without [the
  thing they dread]."
- **The capability for the job.** "The [capability] for your [job to be done]." If the output
  reads generic, the inputs were too broad. That is the formula working, not failing.
- **PAS or BAB.** Problem, agitation, solution. Or before, after, bridge. Both work better
  across a headline-plus-subhead pair than inside a single line.

**Match the awareness stage.** Most homepage visitors are *problem aware*: they feel the pain
and do not yet know a category of solution exists. Writing to a product-aware visitor is the
most common homepage failure. Schwartz's ladder, and what each rung wants:

| Stage | What the hero must do |
|---|---|
| Unaware | Make the problem visible |
| Problem aware | Name the pain in their words |
| Solution aware | Show the mechanism, and how it differs |
| Product aware | Differentiate against the named alternative |
| Most aware | Make the offer |

A homepage usually needs to open problem-aware and reach mechanism by the subhead.

## The audience qualifier trap

A qualifier earns its place only if it **excludes** somebody. "For teams", "for modern teams",
"for growing companies", "for businesses" exclude nobody and cost you the words that could
have carried an outcome. The same is true of a qualifier that describes a *process* rather
than a *person*: it sounds precise, but the reader cannot tell whether they are in it.

Test any qualifier three ways:

1. **Who does this exclude?** No answer means cut it.
2. **Would a reader self-identify in under a second?** A job title, a company shape, or a
   named pain passes. A description of their sales process does not.
3. **Is it doing work the subhead should do?** Qualifiers belong in the subhead more often
   than in the headline.

Specificity is not a risk. Vague copy is not safe copy, it is forgettable copy.

## Checklist before shipping a hero

- [ ] Headline names an outcome or a tension, not a product category
- [ ] Headline is under about 8 words, or has a reason to be longer
- [ ] Subhead adds a new fact and is one sentence
- [ ] Every audience qualifier excludes somebody
- [ ] A stranger could say what this does after one read
- [ ] One primary CTA, visually dominant, with a verb the visitor recognizes
- [ ] Hero visual shows the real product doing the thing
- [ ] At least one trust signal is above the fold
- [ ] No em dashes, no semicolon standing in for a dash, no sentence that needs re-reading

## Benchmarks, for sanity rather than promises

- Self-serve SaaS, warm traffic: **4 to 7%** page conversion is normal.
- Sales-led pages driving a demo booking: **1 to 3%**.
- Strong pages across both: **8 to 15%**.

Quote these as context for a decision, never as a claim on a page.
